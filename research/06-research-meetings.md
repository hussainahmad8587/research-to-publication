# Productive Research Meetings

> Research meetings should clarify thinking, uncover problems, obtain feedback, and produce useful decisions.

## Why Meetings Matter

A meeting is useful when it helps the project move forward.

Progress may include:

- Understanding why an experiment failed.
- Revising a weak assumption.
- Identifying a data problem.
- Choosing between alternative explanations.
- Agreeing on a better experiment.

Positive results are not a requirement for a productive discussion.

## Maintain a Project Document

Keep a concise document containing:

- The research question.
- The intended contribution.
- Current hypotheses.
- Major decisions.
- Important experiment results.
- Unresolved problems.
- Responsibilities.
- Next actions.

Update it before meetings and keep detailed logs available separately.

If you are still exploring a topic, maintain a personal document with reading notes, questions, and early experiments.

## Prepare a Clear Agenda

### 1. Revisit the Objective

State the central question and explain whether the current work still addresses it.

### 2. Review Previous Actions

Report:

- What was completed.
- What changed.
- What remains outstanding.
- Why an action was delayed or revised.

### 3. Present the Main Progress

Focus on a few meaningful developments.

Show evidence and explain what you learned.

### 4. Discuss Obstacles

For each problem, present:

- What happened.
- What you expected.
- What you checked.
- Possible explanations.
- The feedback or decision you need.

### 5. Agree on Next Steps

Record clear tasks, owners, target dates, and completion criteria.

## Bring Analysis With the Problem

### An Unprepared Update

> The model performs poorly. I do not know why.

### A More Useful Update

> Validation performance declined after adding the new input. I checked preprocessing and alignment, compared the image-only baseline, and found that the decline is concentrated in cases with missing metadata. I propose testing explicit missing-value handling.

The second update supports discussion because it connects observations, checks, and a proposed action.

## Discuss Openly and Respectfully

- Ask when an explanation is unclear.
- Present disagreements through reasoning and evidence.
- Explain your own interpretation.
- Listen to alternative explanations.
- Revise your position when the evidence changes.
- Raise important blockers promptly.

You do not need a complete solution before asking for help. You should explain the problem and your current thinking clearly.

## Agree on a Suitable Frequency

Meeting frequency depends on:

- Project stage.
- Supervision arrangements.
- Collaboration needs.
- Urgency.
- The cost of waiting on a decision.

Agree on a regular schedule and communicate significant blockers between meetings when necessary.

A completion criterion makes the task reviewable.

**Vague task:**

> Investigate robustness.

**Clearer task:**

> Compare the baseline and proposed method under three predefined missing-input settings and summarize the results.

## Follow Through

After the meeting:

1. Review the notes while the discussion is fresh.
2. Clarify ambiguous instructions.
3. Update the project plan.
4. Complete or revise the agreed actions.
5. Record why priorities change.

A meeting becomes valuable when its decisions influence the work that follows.

## Common Mistakes

- Treating the meeting as a performance test.
- Hiding failed experiments.
- Sharing raw logs without interpretation.
- Waiting too long to discuss a blocker.
- Agreeing on vague tasks.
- Repeating discussions because no decisions were recorded.

## Before and After Each Meeting

- [ ] I can explain the current objective.
- [ ] I reviewed the previous action list.
- [ ] I selected the most important evidence.
- [ ] I analyzed the main obstacles.
- [ ] I prepared specific questions.
- [ ] Decisions are recorded.
- [ ] Tasks have owners and completion criteria.
- [ ] I know what to do next.
