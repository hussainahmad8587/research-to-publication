# Presenting Your Research

> A good research presentation helps listeners understand the question, follow the reasoning, evaluate the evidence, and join the discussion.

## Start With a Clear Purpose

Identify what the presentation should accomplish.

| Presentation type | Main purpose |
| --- | --- |
| Paper discussion | Explain and assess a published study |
| Topic introduction | Provide an organized overview of a research area |
| Progress update | Discuss findings, obstacles, and next steps |
| Completed study | Communicate the contribution and supporting evidence |

Choose the structure and level of detail accordingly.

## Understand Your Audience

Consider:

- What does the audience already know?
- Which terms require explanation?
- What background is essential?
- What should listeners remember?
- What discussion or decision do you need?

Explain enough to make the argument understandable without overwhelming listeners.

## Design Readable Slides

Use:

- Consistent fonts.
- Clear heading hierarchy.
- Strong text contrast.
- Readable figure labels.
- Short statements.
- Carefully selected visual emphasis.

For many presentation settings, titles around 32–36 points and body text around 24 points or larger provide a useful starting point. Adjust to the display and viewing distance.

Check your slides at realistic size.

Avoid paragraphs that you must read aloud.

## Presenting a Research Paper

### 1. State the Central Message

Summarize the paper in two or three sentences:

> The paper investigates ______. It proposes ______ to address ______. Its main reported evidence is ______.

### 2. Explain the Motivation

Describe:

- The research problem.
- Why it matters.
- What previous approaches accomplish.
- What limitation remains.

### 3. Explain the Method

Begin with an overview.

Trace the flow from input to output, then explain the components necessary to understand the contribution.

For each important component, clarify its purpose and assumptions.

### 4. Evaluate the Evidence

Explain:

- Why the datasets are relevant.
- Whether comparisons are fair.
- What the main results demonstrate.
- Which ablations support the argument.
- Where the approach fails.

### 5. Provide Your Assessment

Distinguish the authors’ claims from your judgment.

Discuss:

- The strongest contribution.
- Important limitations.
- Unanswered questions.
- Possible improvements.
- Relevance to your project.

## Presenting Your Own Progress

A useful sequence is:

1. Research question.
2. Intended contribution.
3. Previous plan.
4. Work completed.
5. Main results.
6. Interpretation.
7. Current obstacles.
8. Questions for discussion.
9. Next actions.

Include negative results when they inform the next decision.

## Explain Figures Clearly

For each figure:

1. State the question it addresses.
2. Explain the axes, labels, or components.
3. Direct attention to the important pattern.
4. State the supported conclusion.
5. Mention relevant limitations.

Give listeners time to inspect the evidence.

## Speak Clearly

- Use straightforward sentences.
- Define abbreviations.
- Keep terminology consistent.
- Make transitions explicit.
- Pause after important points.
- Rehearse within the time limit.

When presenting in a second language, prepare clear explanations rather than trying to memorize every sentence.

## Prepare for Questions

Anticipate questions about:

- Motivation.
- Novelty.
- Assumptions.
- Baseline fairness.
- Data quality.
- Reproducibility.
- Computational cost.
- Failure cases.
- Practical applicability.

If you do not know an answer, explain what is established, what remains uncertain, and how you would investigate it.

## A Simple Presentation Outline

| Section | Main question |
| --- | --- |
| Background | What does the audience need to know? |
| Problem | What remains unresolved? |
| Approach | What did we propose or investigate? |
| Evidence | What supports the findings? |
| Limitations | What do the findings not establish? |
| Discussion | What should happen next? |

## Common Mistakes

- Showing a complex architecture before defining the problem.
- Presenting every result with equal emphasis.
- Using unreadable tables.
- Reporting scores without interpretation.
- Overstating what the evidence establishes.
- Ending without a takeaway or next step.

## Before You Present

- [ ] The purpose is clear.
- [ ] The audience can identify the main question.
- [ ] Each slide has a clear role.
- [ ] Figures are readable.
- [ ] Claims are connected to evidence.
- [ ] Limitations are acknowledged.
- [ ] The presentation fits the time limit.
- [ ] The final slide states the takeaway and next step.
