# Effective Research Habits

> Consistent habits help you preserve knowledge, interpret evidence, avoid repeated mistakes, and make steady progress.

## Build a System You Can Maintain

Your research system should help you answer four questions:

1. What have I read?
2. What have I tried?
3. What have I learned?
4. What should I do next?

Use tools that support these needs without creating unnecessary administrative work.

## 1. Maintain Regular Academic Input

Set aside a short, regular period to scan relevant research.

Possible sources include:

- Selected preprint categories.
- Conference proceedings.
- Journal updates.
- Researcher alerts.
- Curated paper collections.

Use scanning to identify promising work. Reserve separate time for deep reading.

Record why a paper matters before adding it to your reading queue.

## 2. Build a Personal Knowledge System

Organize useful papers and notes in a searchable system.

For each important paper, record:

- The research question.
- The main idea.
- Important evidence.
- Limitations.
- Connections to your project.

Keep quotations, the authors’ conclusions, and your interpretation distinguishable.

Review and update your notes as your understanding develops.

## 3. Keep an Experiment Log

Record important experiments consistently.

| Field | What to record |
| --- | --- |
| Experiment ID | A unique identifier |
| Date | When the experiment ran |
| Question | What you intended to test |
| Main change | What differs from the previous experiment |
| Data | Dataset version and split |
| Preprocessing | Relevant transformations |
| Code | Commit or version |
| Configuration | Model, parameters, and training settings |
| Randomness | Seeds and relevant settings |
| Environment | Dependencies and hardware |
| Results | Metrics and observations |
| Interpretation | What the evidence suggests |
| Next action | What to investigate next |

A random seed alone does not guarantee reproducibility. Preserve data versions, code, dependencies, and relevant environment details.

Record unsuccessful experiments as carefully as successful ones.

## 4. Document Problems and Solutions

When a technical problem occurs, record:

- The symptom.
- The relevant error message.
- The conditions that trigger it.
- What you checked.
- What you tried.
- The established cause, if known.
- The solution.
- How you verified the fix.

If the problem remains unresolved, identify the next diagnostic step.

This record helps both your future self and collaborators.

## 5. Capture Ideas Promptly

Keep a simple idea list.

Each entry should contain:

- The question.
- What triggered it.
- Why it may matter.
- Relevant references.
- A possible first test.

Review the list periodically.

Merge related ideas, discard weak ones, and develop promising questions into short project briefs.

## 6. Test Important Assumptions Early

A small, well-designed experiment can prevent weeks of unnecessary work.

For an initial test:

1. Identify the central assumption.
2. Choose suitable data.
3. Establish a meaningful baseline.
4. Change a clearly defined factor.
5. Evaluate consistently.
6. Decide whether to continue, revise, or stop.

Reuse trustworthy components where appropriate, but verify that they match your intended setting.

## 7. Communicate Before Problems Become Expensive

Discuss uncertainties and unexpected findings with supervisors, peers, or relevant specialists.

Before asking for help:

- Perform basic checks.
- Describe the problem precisely.
- Explain what you have tried.
- Present your current interpretation.
- State what feedback you need.

You do not need to solve everything independently before starting a useful conversation.

## 8. Develop Research Independence

Take responsibility for:

- Proposing next steps.
- Checking assumptions.
- Evaluating evidence.
- Following through.
- Recognizing when expertise or feedback is needed.

Supervision helps improve judgment and direction.

Over time, aim to explain both what you plan to do and why it is the most informative next step.

## 9. Review Progress Weekly

Ask:

- What did I learn?
- Which uncertainty did I reduce?
- What changed my interpretation?
- Which tasks remain incomplete?
- What is blocking progress?
- What is the most valuable next action?

Measure progress through improved understanding and reliable evidence, alongside completed tasks.

## Common Mistakes

- Saving information without making it retrievable.
- Changing experiments without recording the changes.
- Keeping only positive results.
- Delaying small validation experiments.
- Working alone on a blocker for too long.
- Building elaborate systems that are rarely used.
- Confusing constant activity with meaningful progress.

## Weekly Checklist

- [ ] Reading notes are organized.
- [ ] Important experiments are documented.
- [ ] Code and configurations are preserved.
- [ ] Problems and attempted fixes are recorded.
- [ ] Promising ideas have a possible next test.
- [ ] Necessary questions have been discussed.
- [ ] Decisions and changes in direction are documented.
- [ ] Next week’s priorities are explicit.
